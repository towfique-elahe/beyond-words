\chapter{RESULTS}
\label{ch:results}

All numbers are test macro-F1 unless stated otherwise. Every table and figure
in this chapter is computed from the per-clip test predictions released with
the code.

\section{Clip-level results}
\label{sec:res-clip}

On the canonical clip-level split, frozen Whisper-small embeddings with an MLP
head reach a macro-F1 of \fscore{0.917} (accuracy 0.916, $n = 1{,}329$), and
end-to-end fine-tuned XLS-R-300M reaches \fscore{0.952}, 3.5 points higher.
The MFCC baseline reaches \fscore{0.727}. \Cref{tab:clip-perclass} lists the
per-class scores of the three models, and \cref{fig:perclass-clip} plots the
two pretrained models side by side; \cref{fig:perclass-baseline} in
\cref{app:figures} repeats the frozen baseline's scores sorted by value. Khulna,
Sylhet and Rajshahi are the easiest classes for every model, and Noakhali,
Mymensingh, Chattogram and Barishal the hardest.

Three observations follow.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Fine-tuning's gains concentrate on the frozen baseline's weakest classes:
        Noakhali (+6.1 points), Dhaka (+5.9), Barishal (+5.6) and Chattogram
        (+4.4). Task adaptation moves capacity towards the hard, acoustically
        close dialects.
  \item The dominant confusion is between Barishal and Noakhali, two southern
        coastal dialects. Fine-tuning halves it but does not remove it.
        Mymensingh, a transitional dialect zone, spreads its errors across its
        neighbours. Errors follow dialect geography rather than falling at
        random.
  \item Training converges: the 8-epoch pilot reached 0.865 and was still
        rising, while the 20-epoch run's validation score plateaus at about 0.95
        from epoch 11.
\end{itemize}

\Cref{fig:confusion} shows the test confusion matrices of the two pretrained
models, with true classes in rows and predictions in columns. In the frozen
model's matrix the Barishal-Noakhali cell pair holds $14 + 17 = 31$ errors; in
the fine-tuned model's it holds $10 + 5 = 15$. The other off-diagonal mass sits
between Mymensingh and its neighbours and between the Standard class and the
regional classes whose clips contain stretches of near-standard speech.
\Cref{fig:training-curve} shows the XLS-R training run itself: the training
loss falls steadily over the 20 epochs while validation macro-F1 rises quickly
to about 0.9 by epoch 5 and flattens near 0.95 from epoch 11, which is the
evidence that the lengthened schedule was sufficient.

\section{Strict-split results: quantifying leakage}
\label{sec:res-strict}

\Cref{tab:ablation} crosses the three representations with the two split
protocols and adds the augmentation variant of \cref{sec:method-acoustic};
\cref{fig:ablation} plots the same numbers as paired bars, which makes the size
of each drop visible at a glance. Every representation loses accuracy under the
strict split, and the hand-crafted features lose the most.

    Frozen Whisper-small + MLP     & 0.917 & 0.793 & $-12.4$ \\
    Fine-tuned XLS-R-300M          & 0.952 & 0.808 & $-14.4$ \\
    XLS-R-300M + channel augmentation & n/a & 0.748 & n/a \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{ablation_representation_split.pdf}
  \caption{Test macro-F1 per representation under both split protocols.}
  \label{fig:ablation}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Leakage is measured at 12--14 macro-F1 points} for the
        pretrained models, and about 25 for MFCC features. The strict numbers,
        0.793 and 0.808, are the honest estimates; clip-level numbers are kept
        for comparability with prior work.
  \item \textbf{Under the strict split, fine-tuned and frozen models are
        statistically tied.} XLS-R's 1.5-point lead over frozen Whisper has a
        speaker-group bootstrap 95\,\% confidence interval of $-2.0$ to $+7.5$,
        whereas its 3.5-point clip-level lead ($+1.8$ to $+5.3$) is significant.
        Fine-tuning's strict-split drop ($-14.4$) is slightly larger than the
        frozen model's ($-12.4$).
  \item \textbf{Per-class strict scores are noisier than the macro average.}
        Some strict test cells are dominated by a single large pseudo-speaker
        cluster: Barishal's strict test set is essentially one 251-clip
        cluster, so its strict F1 of 0.603 (Whisper) and 0.508 (XLS-R)
        measures generalisation to one speaker or source more than to the
        class.
\end{enumerate}

\Cref{tab:strict-perclass} gives the per-class strict scores behind the third
observation. The two pretrained models fail differently under the strict split:
frozen Whisper is better on Standard, Dhaka and Rajshahi, fine-tuned XLS-R on
Chattogram, Noakhali, Mymensingh and Khulna. Their macro averages are close,
but they are not the same model with different accuracy.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{data_efficiency.pdf}
  \caption{Data efficiency of frozen Whisper and MFCC features.}
  \label{fig:efficiency}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Whisper embeddings trained on 10\,\% of the labels outperform MFCC
        features trained on all of them}, under both protocols. Pretraining is
        worth more than a tenfold increase in labelled data on this task.
  \item The Whisper curves flatten beyond about 50\,\%: the 13-hour corpus is
        adequately sized for the frozen-embedding approach.
  \item The gap between representations is wider under the strict split at every
        fraction, so the pretrained features' robustness to leakage holds across
        data scales.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Augmentation study}
\label{sec:res-augment}

Training the strict-split XLS-R model with the channel-suppression chain of
\cref{sec:method-acoustic} \emph{reduced} test macro-F1 from 0.808 to 0.748. The
validation score was depressed throughout training (best epoch 17 of 20,
validation 0.702 against 0.757 without augmentation), so the drop is not an
artefact of checkpoint selection. \Cref{sec:disc-augment} interprets it.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Whisper-medium family sets a new honest headline of
        0.92.} Fine-tuned Whisper-medium reaches 0.919 under the strict split
        and Tugstugi (Ben-10) 0.921, against 0.808 for XLS-R. The gap of
        $+11.1$ points for Whisper-medium has a speaker-group interval of
        $+1.5$ to $+16.8$ ($P(\text{gap} \le 0) = 0.005$) and Tugstugi's
        $+10.0$ an interval of $+0.7$ to $+14.4$; Tugstugi (Ben-10)'s $+11.2$
        is borderline ($-0.6$ to $+16.3$, $P = 0.04$), because its strict
        interval is widened by the single-cluster Barishal cell. At clip level
        all three gains ($+2.9$ to $+3.3$) are significant. The intervals are
        wide because the strict test set has 230 speaker groups.
  \item \textbf{Once fine-tuned, the three Whisper-medium checkpoints are
        statistically indistinguishable.} Their pairwise strict gaps are
        $-0.2$, $+1.1$ and $+1.3$ points, every interval straddling zero (e.g.\
        Whisper-medium vs Tugstugi (Ben-10): $-4.7$ to $+2.0$). Frozen, the
        Bangla-adapted encoder is clearly better: Tugstugi (Ben-10) scores
        0.857 strict against 0.782 for frozen Whisper-medium ($+7.5$, interval
        $+0.4$ to $+12.3$), and frozen Whisper-medium is no better than frozen
        Whisper-small (0.782 vs 0.793). The Bangla speech-recognition and
        dialect adaptations therefore help a frozen encoder, but add nothing
        measurable once the encoder is fine-tuned on the dialect task.
  \item \textbf{Fine-tuning helps on the same checkpoint.} Whisper-medium
        goes from 0.782 frozen to 0.919 fine-tuned under the strict split
        ($+13.8$, interval $+6.5$ to $+22.3$) and from 0.919 to 0.984 at clip
        level ($+6.5$, interval $+5.2$ to $+8.5$); Tugstugi (Ben-10) from 0.857
        to 0.921 ($+6.4$, interval $+3.6$ to $+10.4$) and from 0.936 to 0.980
        ($+4.4$, interval $+3.3$ to $+6.4$). The earlier comparison of frozen
        Whisper-small with fine-tuned XLS-R (\cref{sec:res-strict}), which found
        no significant strict-split difference, therefore mixed the effect of
        the regime with that of the encoder.
  \item \textbf{Fine-tuning a transcription-pretrained encoder halves the
        leakage drop.} The fine-tuned Whisper-medium family loses 6.0--7.3
        points from the clip-level to the strict split, against 12.4--13.8 for
        the frozen Whisper encoders and 14.4 for fine-tuned XLS-R. For
        Whisper-medium the drop falls from 13.8 frozen to 6.5 fine-tuned, so
        the robustness is produced by fine-tuning the encoder, not inherited
        from it; XLS-R's fine-tuning did not have this effect. The
        Bangla-adapted Tugstugi (Ben-10) is already robust when frozen
        (drop 7.9).
  \item \textbf{Hishab's FastConformer is the weakest pretrained model} at
        0.746 strict and 0.901 clip-level, below frozen Whisper-small and 6.2
        points below XLS-R under the strict split (interval $-14.7$ to $-0.2$).
        Its encoder is a quarter of the size (115M parameters, 18 layers of
        width 512), was trained with a CTC objective on broadcast news, and
        reached its best validation score at epoch 18 of 20, so it may also be
        under-trained; its drop of 15.5 points is the largest of any pretrained
        model.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The embedding space before and after fine-tuning}
\label{sec:res-tsne}

t-SNE~\cite{Maaten2008Visualizing}, once from the frozen Whisper-small encoder

\subsection{How much dialect survives transcription?}
\label{sec:res-asr}

\Cref{tab:asr-quality} scores the five front-ends against the human transcripts
of Ben-10's validation set (1,666 clips from ten districts, greedy decoding,
punctuation ignored), which contain 21,119 reference tokens outside the
standard vocabulary.

  \label{tab:asr-quality}

    \toprule
    Front-end & WER & CER & Dialect-word recall & Standard-word recall \\
    \midrule
    Tugstugi (Ben-10)      & \textbf{0.645} & \textbf{0.333} & \textbf{0.266} & \textbf{0.620} \\
    Tugstugi               & 0.752 & 0.484 & 0.040 & 0.416 \\

The ordering of the error rates matches the Ben-10 benchmark
\cite{Dipto2025ASR}, which reports 0.70 for the Ben-10-fine-tuned model on
its private test set and 0.81, 0.87 and 1.13 for Tugstugi, Hishab and
Whisper-large-v3; the validation-set values here run a little lower for the
Whisper models. Even the best front-end reproduces only about one dialect word
in four; by district its recall ranges from 0.364 (Sylhet) to 0.173
(Kishoreganj). The standard-Bangla models reproduce 1--4\,\% of dialect words.
The benchmark's hand-counted recall for the two Tugstugi models (0.16--0.53
against 0.01--0.09) is thus confirmed at full scale with an automated measure
(per district 0.17--0.36 against 0.01--0.14).

\Cref{fig:asr-dialect} summarises what survives transcription. Panel (a) plots
the dialect-word recall of \cref{tab:asr-quality}, where the Ben-10 model stands
alone at 0.27 and the other four lie below 0.05. Panel (b) turns to the thesis
corpus, which has no references, and shows the share of transcript words
outside the standard vocabulary for Standard clips and for the mean over the
eight regional classes. Only the Ben-10 model separates regional clips (25\,\%
on average) from Standard clips (8\,\%) by a wide margin, with recognisable
dialect forms such as \bn{mui}{মুই}, \bn{ai}{আঁই}, \bn{ar}{আঁর},
\bn{hamar}{হামার}, \bn{kita}{কিতা} and \bn{teha}{টেহা}. Hishab and the base
Tugstugi model output almost as few non-standard words on regional clips (9\,\%
and 13\,\%) as on Standard ones (4\,\% and 5\,\%). The wav2vec~2.0 and
Whisper-large-v3 front-ends have high shares for the wrong reason:
misrecognition, and for Whisper-large-v3 repetition loops in 12.3\,\% of
transcripts (one word repeated five or more times in a row) and 2.5--4.8\,\%
empty outputs per class.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Word matching is weak} even with the best front-end: 0.360
        strict and 0.399 clip-level. Fewer than half of the strict test clips
        (44\,\%) contain any lexicon word, so the rest default to Standard.
        Relaxing the lexicon thresholds raises coverage to 59\,\% and macro-F1
        to 0.419, still far below every pretrained acoustic model. The clean
        external lexicon (Ben-10 and Vashantor; six classes including Standard)
        scores 0.418 on its own classes with 51\,\% coverage. With the
        standard-Bangla front-ends, coverage collapses to 2--5\,\% and matching
        fails: there are no regional words left to match.
  \item \textbf{The TF-IDF classifier roughly doubles the matching score}
        (0.633 strict with the Ben-10 model), placing the words alone between
        MFCC features (0.480) and frozen Whisper (0.793). The front-ends rank
        exactly as their word error rates on Ben-10 do
        (\cref{tab:asr-quality}).
  \item \textbf{Fusion improves on XLS-R with four of the five front-ends.} With
        the Ben-10 model the strict score rises from 0.808 to 0.856
        ($\alpha = 0.25$, so after tuning the text carries three quarters of the
        log-probability weight) and the clip-level score from 0.952 to 0.967
        ($\alpha = 0.30$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Fusion with the stronger acoustic model}
\label{sec:res-fusion-wm}

The fusion above was built on XLS-R. Repeating it with fine-tuned
Whisper-medium (\cref{sec:res-encoders}) as the acoustic partner, with the
same TF-IDF model, the same Ben-10 transcripts and $\alpha$ re-tuned on
validation, gives \cref{tab:fusion-wm}, which places the two acoustic partners
side by side under each protocol. Under the strict split the words no longer
help: fusion scores 0.909 against 0.919 for Whisper-medium alone, a loss of 1.1
points whose speaker-group interval ($-2.5$ to $-0.03$) just excludes zero.
Fusion changes 102 of the 1,327 test predictions, repairing 38 and breaking 50.
The validation score rises (0.803 to 0.847), which is why $\alpha = 0.25$ is
selected, but the gain does not transfer to the test speakers. The classes
XLS-R found hardest and the words repaired, Barishal and Sylhet, are already at
0.88--0.94 for Whisper-medium, so there is little left for the text to fix. At
clip level the fusion is a null result: 0.984 against 0.984, with five
predictions changed, two repaired and two broken.

\section{Comparative analysis with prior studies}
\label{sec:res-comparative}

\Cref{tab:comparative} sets this thesis beside the published work it builds on
or is closest to. The studies differ in task, language, data and metric, so the
table compares designs and limitations rather than numbers; the two Bangla
accent-classification studies that gathered the corpus used by this thesis are
the direct predecessors. Read down the last column, the table shows the three
things this thesis adds to each line of work: pretrained encoders in place of
hand-crafted features, a measured leakage correction in place of a random
split, and a lexical route whose text comes from speech recognition rather than
from human writing.
