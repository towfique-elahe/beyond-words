\chapter{METHODOLOGY}
\label{ch:method}

\section{Task formulation}

Dialect identification is posed as 9-way single-label classification: given a
clip of about five seconds, predict one of Barishal, Chattogram, Dhaka, Khulna,
Mymensingh, Noakhali, Rajshahi, Standard or Sylhet. The headline metric is
macro-averaged F1 on the test split, which weights every class equally despite
the imbalance in \cref{tab:classes}; chance level is about 0.11. Every model is
evaluated under the two split protocols of \cref{sec:method-splits}, and every
headline number carries a bootstrap confidence interval
(\cref{sec:method-eval}).

\Cref{fig:pipeline} shows the two routes compared in this thesis. The acoustic
route (\cref{sec:method-acoustic}) classifies the audio directly: a 5\,s clip
enters a hand-crafted feature extractor, a frozen pretrained encoder or a
fine-tuned encoder, and a classification head returns class probabilities. The
lexical route (\cref{sec:method-lexical}) transcribes the clip with Bangla
speech recognition first and classifies the words, either by matching them
against regional word lists or with a text classifier. Both routes share the
splits, the metric and the evaluation code, and their class probabilities are
combined by late fusion (\cref{eq:fusion}), so that the question of the title,
words or sound, can be answered on the same test clips.

\section{Splitting protocols}
\label{sec:method-splits}

Two protocols are used throughout. \Cref{fig:split-protocols} shows how each
is built and \cref{tab:splits} gives the resulting sizes. In the clip-level
split, drawn on the left of the figure, only the 557 Standard clips with a
known source are kept source-disjoint; every other clip is assigned
individually, so the same speaker may appear on both sides. In the strict
split, drawn on the right, every clip is first embedded with a speaker model
and clustered into pseudo-speakers, and whole clusters are then assigned to
train, validation or test, so that no group crosses the three parts. The two
protocols produce almost the same number of clips in each part, as
\cref{tab:splits} shows, which keeps the scores under them comparable.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{split-protocols.pdf}
  \caption{The two split protocols.}
  \label{fig:split-protocols}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Clip-level split.} The clip-level split is a stratified 75/10/15
train/validation/test split drawn with seed 42. Clips with a known source
(the 557 sourced Standard clips) are split so that no source crosses the three
parts; all other clips are split individually. Because the same speaker or
recording can then appear on both sides, scores under this protocol are an
\emph{upper bound}. The split is committed to the repository for
reproducibility.

\paragraph{Strict pseudo-speaker split.} Each clip is embedded with the
SpeechBrain ECAPA-TDNN speaker model \cite{Desplanques2020ECAPA}, which
produces a 192-dimensional speaker vector. Within each class the embeddings are
clustered by agglomerative clustering with cosine distance and average linkage,
with no preset number of clusters. The distance threshold is not hand-picked:
it is \emph{calibrated on the Standard class}, whose true sources are known
for 557 clips, by sweeping 0.30--0.90 and keeping the threshold that maximises
the adjusted Rand index \cite{Hubert1985Comparing} between clusters and true
sources. The best threshold is 0.50, with ARI 0.347. Clustering every class at
this threshold yields 1,683 pseudo-speaker groups, and a grouped split then
guarantees that no group crosses train, validation and test (verified: 0 of
1,683).

The modest calibration ARI is acknowledged: pseudo-speakers only partly recover
true sources, so the strict split removes much, but not provably all, of the
speaker and channel overlap. It stands in for true speaker labels, which the
corpus lacks. Standard is not a clean control either: only its 557 sourced
clips are source-disjoint under both protocols, while its other 1,095 clips
move from clip-level splitting to pseudo-speaker grouping like the regional
classes.

\section{Acoustic models}
\label{sec:method-acoustic}

All acoustic models share one protocol: inverse-frequency class-weighted
cross-entropy, model selection by validation macro-F1, and seed 42.
\Cref{fig:acoustic-models} shows the three configurations compared: (a)
hand-crafted MFCC statistics with an MLP head, (b) a frozen pretrained encoder
whose mean-pooled hidden states feed the same MLP head, and (c) an end-to-end
fine-tuned encoder with a pooled classification head, in which the encoder's
Transformer layers are trained together with the head while its convolutional
front end stays frozen. Models (a) to (c) are the original comparison; (d) and
(e) below are further instances of configuration (c), added after a supervisor
review to place Bangla-pretrained encoders beside the multilingual ones and to
fine-tune Whisper as XLS-R was fine-tuned.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{acoustic-models.pdf}
  \caption{The three acoustic model configurations.}
  \label{fig:acoustic-models}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{(a) MFCC statistics + MLP.} Forty MFCCs with their first and second
differences are mean- and standard-deviation pooled over time into a
240-dimensional vector, fed to the same head as (b). This re-implements the
spirit of the project's original hand-crafted pipeline under the current
protocol, so that representations are compared fairly.

\paragraph{(b) Frozen Whisper-small + MLP.} Each clip is encoded once by the
frozen encoder of Whisper-small; hidden states are mean-pooled to 768
dimensions and cached. The classifier is a two-hidden-layer MLP
(768--256--256--9, ReLU, dropout 0.3) trained with AdamW
\cite{Loshchilov2019Decoupled} (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$)
for 60 full-batch epochs on CPU, which makes it bit-reproducible, keeping the
checkpoint with the best validation macro-F1.

\paragraph{(c) Fine-tuned XLS-R-300M.} The 300-million-parameter XLS-R
checkpoint \cite{Babu2022XLSR} with a sequence-classification head; the
convolutional feature encoder is frozen and the Transformer and head are
fine-tuned. Two learning-rate groups (encoder $2\times10^{-5}$, head
$10^{-4}$), 10\,\% linear warm-up then linear decay, batch 8 with gradient
accumulation 2 (effective batch 16), fp16 mixed precision, gradient clipping at
1.0, 20 epochs. Inputs are 5\,s waveforms, padded or trimmed and normalised per
utterance. An 8-epoch pilot (macro-F1 0.865, still rising at the last epoch)
showed that the schedule needed lengthening; at 20 epochs validation plateaus
from about epoch 11, as \cref{fig:training-curve} in \cref{sec:res-clip} shows.

\paragraph{(d) Fine-tuned Whisper-medium encoders.} The same recipe as (c),
applied to the encoder of three Whisper-medium checkpoints: the multilingual
Whisper-medium \cite{Radford2023Robust}, and the two Bangla speech-recognition
models of \cref{tab:asr-fronts} that share its architecture, \emph{Tugstugi}
(fine-tuned on standard Bangla by Bengali.AI) and \emph{Tugstugi (Ben-10)}
(further fine-tuned on dialect speech). Each is loaded as an encoder-only
classifier: the sequence-to-sequence checkpoint's decoder is dropped, and a
projector, mean-pooling over time and a linear classifier are added, mirroring
the XLS-R head (the audio-classification variant of Whisper in the Transformers
library \cite{Wolf2020Transformers}). Whisper's encoder is built for 30\,s of
input; to train on 5\,s clips at a sixth of the cost, only the first 250 rows
of its (frozen) positional table are kept, which corresponds to 500 log-mel
frames, and the two convolutional input layers are frozen as XLS-R's feature
encoder is. All 24 Transformer layers (302M parameters) and the head are
trained with the hyperparameters of (c), for 12 epochs rather than 20 because
XLS-R's validation curve had plateaued by epoch 11 and the larger encoder
converges faster; a run takes about 70 minutes on a T4. Raw amplitude is fed to
Whisper's own log-mel front end; the per-utterance normalisation of (c) is not
applied.

\paragraph{(e) Fine-tuned FastConformer.} Hishab's Bangla FastConformer
\cite{Hishab2023FastConformer}, released as an NVIDIA NeMo model, is the
Bangla-pretrained counterpart of (d) from a different architecture. Its mel
front end and Conformer encoder are taken from the released ASR model, the
encoder output is mean-pooled over the unpadded frames and a linear classifier
is added. The encoder and head are trained with the hyperparameters of (c) for
20 epochs; the encoder is much smaller than Whisper-medium's.

\paragraph{Frozen rows for (d).} To separate the effect of fine-tuning from
that of the encoder, the frozen recipe of (b) is also run on the Whisper-medium
and Tugstugi (Ben-10) encoders, so that the same checkpoint appears both frozen
and fine-tuned.

\paragraph{Augmentation variant.} A train-only waveform augmentation chain
aimed at recording-channel signatures: gain $\pm 6$\,dB ($p=0.5$), Gaussian
noise at 10--30\,dB SNR ($p=0.5$), seven-band parametric equalisation
$\pm 6$\,dB ($p=0.3$), telephone-style band-pass 300--3400\,Hz ($p=0.15$),
pitch shift $\pm 2$ semitones ($p=0.25$), and time stretch 0.9--1.1
($p=0.25$).

\section{Evaluation and statistical uncertainty}
\label{sec:method-eval}

Every headline number is one seed on one test set. To separate signal from
sampling noise, the bootstrap procedure resamples the saved test predictions
$B = 2{,}000$ times in two ways: resampling clips, and resampling whole
pseudo-speaker or source groups. The group bootstrap is the honest choice,
because clips of one speaker are correlated and a clip bootstrap understates
the variance \cite{Efron1993Introduction}. For each model it reports the 95\,\%
percentile interval of macro-F1, and for each pair of models the interval of
the difference and the proportion of resamples in which the difference is zero
or negative. The clip-level test set contains 565 groups and the strict test
set 230.

\section{Data-efficiency protocol}
\label{sec:method-efficiency}

The data-efficiency protocol retrains the MLP head of (a) and (b) on
stratified fractions of 10, 25, 50, 75 and 100\,\% of the training split,
three seeds per point, under both protocols; evaluation is always on the full,
fixed test split. Fine-tuning is excluded because each point would cost a full
GPU run.

\section{The lexical route}
\label{sec:method-lexical}

The acoustic models never observe \emph{what} is said. The lexical route asks
how much dialect identity is recoverable from the words alone and whether it
adds to the acoustics. It reuses the splits, the metric and the bootstrap
unchanged, so every number is directly comparable with
\cref{sec:method-acoustic}. \Cref{fig:lexical-route} gives the overview: an
off-the-shelf recogniser transcribes the clip, the text is normalised and
tokenised, and the tokens then take one of two paths. The first filters the
transcript to words outside the standard vocabulary and matches them against
regional word lists, which needs no training; the second feeds the whole
transcript to a TF-IDF classifier, whose probabilities are fused with the
acoustic model's.

\subsection{Transcription}

The corpus has no transcripts, so text is produced by off-the-shelf Bangla
speech recognition. The recognisers follow the Ben-10 benchmark
\cite{Dipto2025ASR} as far as public checkpoints allow. \Cref{tab:asr-fronts}
lists the five front-ends with their publishers and training data; their public
identifiers are in \cref{tab:checkpoints}. \textbf{None was trained or
fine-tuned in this work}; each is used as released. Google's paid recogniser
and the benchmark's Ben-10-fine-tuned wav2vec~2.0, which has no public
checkpoint, are omitted. The five span the range that matters for the lexical
route: two were trained on standard Bangla only, one of those was then
fine-tuned on dialect speech, one is a Bangla model trained on broadcast news,
and one is a multilingual model used zero-shot.

Decoding is greedy throughout. For the Whisper models, the language and task
are fixed to Bangla transcription with no prompt and a cap of 128 new tokens on
the 5\,s thesis clips (median output 53 tokens; 0.2\,\% of clips reach 120) and
440 on Ben-10's up-to-30\,s clips; the two Tugstugi checkpoints ship without a
language-token table, so the generation configuration of the base multilingual
Whisper-medium is substituted, which changes no weights. The wav2vec~2.0 model
is decoded by CTC argmax \cite{Graves2006Connectionist} without a language
model; the FastConformer is run through NVIDIA NeMo \cite{Kuchaiev2019NeMo}.
All 9,303 clips were transcribed on a Kaggle T4 GPU (43, 45, 82, 15 and 7
minutes for the five front-ends in the order of \cref{tab:asr-fronts}). A
450-clip pilot run on an Apple M1 agreed with the T4 output on 93.6\,\% of
clips; all reported numbers use the T4 transcripts.

\subsection{Normalisation and the standard-Bangla vocabulary}

All text (transcripts, reference corpora and word lists) passes one tokeniser:
Unicode NFC normalisation, Bangla-letter tokens only (digits, punctuation and
Latin script are dropped), word-level normalisation with a public Bangla
Unicode normaliser, and removal of zero-width joiners.

The \emph{standard vocabulary} $V$ is the word inventory of OOD-Speech's
963,636 training sentences \cite{Rakib2023OODSpeech}: 8.19 million tokens and
156,643 word types, of which the 88,262 types occurring at least three times are
kept to exclude typos. A transcript word outside $V$ is a \emph{candidate
regional word}. The Ben-10 paper characterises its districts by the same
out-of-vocabulary construction.

\subsection{Regional lexicons}

A lexicon maps each region $r$ to a set of words $w$ with a weight
$p(r \mid w)$. With $c_r(w)$ the count of $w$ in region $r$'s text, $N_r$ the
total number of tokens in that text, and add-$\tfrac12$ smoothing,
\begin{equation}
  p(r \mid w) = \frac{(c_r(w) + \tfrac12)/N_r}{\sum_{q} (c_q(w) + \tfrac12)/N_q},
  \label{eq:lexicon-weight}
\end{equation}
so that a word shared by several dialects counts less towards each. Two
lexicons are built:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \textbf{external lexicon} comes from human-written dialect text
        independent of the corpus: the Ben-10 training transcripts and the
        regional side of Vashantor (\cref{sec:data-external}). A Vashantor word
        that also appears on the standard side of the same sentence pair is not
        counted as regional. Districts are matched by exact name only, so the
        external lexicon covers Barishal, Chattogram, Sylhet, Noakhali and
        Mymensingh.
  \item The \textbf{in-domain lexicon} comes from the speech-recognition
        transcripts of the \emph{training split only}; it is verified in code
        that no validation or test clip contributes. A candidate regional word
        is kept for a class if it occurs at least three times across at least
        two pseudo-speakers of that class, which suppresses recognition noise
        and programme-specific words. It covers all classes and is built
        separately for each front-end and each split protocol.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Classifiers}

Three classifiers operate on the transcripts.

\paragraph{Word matching.} For a clip with tokens $t_1, \dots, t_n$, the
\emph{non-standard share} is $f = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \mathbb{1}[t_i \notin V]$,
and the \emph{match percentage} for each region is
\begin{equation}
  s_r = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} p(r \mid t_i),
  \label{eq:match}
\end{equation}
where $p(r \mid t_i) = 0$ for words absent from region $r$'s lexicon. A clip
with $f < \tau$, or with $s_r = 0$ for every region, is classified as Standard;
otherwise it is assigned to $\arg\max_r s_r$. The threshold $\tau$ is chosen on
the validation split. The method needs no training, and the per-word evidence
behind each decision can be shown to a user.

\paragraph{TF-IDF + logistic regression.} The normalised transcript is
represented by TF-IDF features over word 1--2-grams and character 2--5-grams
(sublinear term frequency, minimum document frequency 2), and classified by
logistic regression with balanced class weights, with the regularisation
strength $C \in \{0.3, 1, 3, 10, 30\}$ chosen on validation
\cite{Pedregosa2011Scikit}. Unlike word matching, it uses standard words too.

\paragraph{Late fusion.} With $p_\text{a}$ the class probabilities of the
fine-tuned XLS-R model and $p_\text{l}$ those of the TF-IDF model,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y} = \arg\max_{c}\; \alpha \log p_\text{a}(c) + (1 - \alpha) \log p_\text{l}(c),
  \label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha \in \{0, 0.05, \dots, 1\}$ chosen on validation. XLS-R
probabilities are computed from the Phase~2 and Phase~3 checkpoints on the same
5\,s inputs used in training; their arg\,max reproduces the saved test
predictions exactly on the strict split and on 1,328 of 1,329 clip-level clips,
the one difference coming from M1 versus T4 arithmetic.

\subsection{Speech-recognition reference evaluation}
\label{sec:method-ben10}

The corpus has no reference transcripts, so recognition quality is measured on
Ben-10's public validation set (1,666 clips from ten districts). Besides
corpus-level word and character error rates on normalised tokens
\cite{Jitsi2024JiWER}, \emph{dialect-word recall} is the proportion of
reference tokens outside $V$ that the hypothesis reproduces exactly (counts
matched per clip). It is the full-set, automated counterpart of the Ben-10
paper's hand-counted dialect recall over about 50 words per district.
\emph{Standard-word recall} is the same measure for reference tokens inside
$V$. On the corpus itself, which has no references, the share of transcript
tokens outside $V$ per class is reported for each front-end.

\section{Compute and reproducibility}

Development and all experiments except fine-tuning and transcription ran on an
Apple M1 laptop (16\,GB, PyTorch MPS backend). Fine-tuning ran on Kaggle's free
T4 GPU tier: about 2.5 hours per XLS-R run, 55--75 minutes per Phase 6 run
(about 9 hours for the eight), and about 4.5 hours in all for the lexical
route's transcription. The pipeline survived a mid-project hardware change (GTX
1660 Ti to M1) with the frozen-embedding baseline reproducing within 0.3
macro-F1 points. The code uses PyTorch \cite{Paszke2019PyTorch}, Hugging Face
Transformers \cite{Wolf2020Transformers}, SpeechBrain
\cite{Ravanelli2021SpeechBrain}, librosa \cite{McFee2015librosa} and
scikit-learn \cite{Pedregosa2011Scikit}. \Cref{app:repro} maps every reported
number to the command that produces it, and \cref{tab:settings} in
\cref{app:settings} lists every training and evaluation setting in one table.
