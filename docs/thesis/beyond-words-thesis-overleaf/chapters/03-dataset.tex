\chapter{DATASET}
\label{ch:dataset}

\section{Source and composition}

The corpus was gathered for two earlier studies of Bangla accent
classification, Badhon \etal~\cite{Badhon2020Bengali} and Alam
\etal~\cite{Alam2023Bangla}, and is used here with the same class labels. It
consists of short clips of Bangla speech cut from television shows, films and
online video, originally distributed as MP4 and grouped into one folder per
class. It contains nine classes: eight regional dialects named after the
district or division they are associated with, and the standard register,
labelled Standard. After validation, 9,303 clips totalling about 13.1 hours are
usable; two duplicate files in the Standard folder are excluded.

\Cref{fig:data-pipeline} summarises how the raw clips become the committed
splits used by every experiment. The raw media clips are first validated and
standardised to one audio format, then described in a manifest that records
every clip's class, duration and, where available, its source identifier, and
finally split under two protocols, a clip-level split and a strict
pseudo-speaker split, both of which are committed to the code repository so
that every experiment reads the same files. \Cref{sec:method-splits} describes
the two protocols.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{data-pipeline.pdf}
  \caption{Data pipeline from raw clips to committed splits.}
  \label{fig:data-pipeline}
\end{figure}

\Cref{tab:classes} lists the classes with their clip counts and the number of
test clips under each protocol, and \cref{fig:class-sizes} shows the same
balance in clips and in hours. Class sizes range from 750 clips (Khulna) to
1,652 (Standard). Standard is the largest class in both measures, and the eight
regional classes range from about one hour of audio (Khulna, Dhaka) to 1.75
hours (Barishal). The imbalance is moderate, about 2.2 to 1 between the largest
and the smallest class, which is why the headline metric of this thesis is
macro-averaged F1 rather than accuracy (\cref{ch:method}).

\section{Audio properties}

Every clip is standardised to 16\,kHz, mono, 16-bit PCM WAV during the
validation step. Clips are nominally five seconds long: the median duration is
5.0\,s and the mean 5.08\,s, but 1,067 clips fall outside 4.5-5.5\,s, with a
range of 1.0-8.0\,s; Standard clips are slightly longer (median 5.6\,s). Five
seconds is enough for a listener to recognise many dialects but excludes
longer-range cues such as discourse markers and phrase-level intonation. Because
the material is broadcast and online media, recording conditions vary widely
within and across classes, from studio news to outdoor drama scenes.

\Cref{fig:durations} shows the duration distribution as a histogram over all
clips, on a logarithmic count axis, and as one box plot per class. Every
regional clip is exactly 5.0\,s, so the spread in panel (a) and the only visible
box in panel (b) come from the Standard class, whose clips were cut to sentence
boundaries rather than to a fixed length. Clip length is therefore a weak but
real cue to the Standard class, which the acoustic models could in principle
exploit; the fixed 5\,s input window of \cref{sec:method-acoustic} removes most
of it.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{dataset_durations.pdf}
  \caption{Clip-duration distribution overall and per class.}

\Cref{fig:spectrograms} shows one held-out clip per class as a waveform and an
80-band log-mel spectrogram covering 0-8\,kHz over the 5\,s of the clip, the
representation Whisper consumes. The examples already hint at the variation the
models face: the Barishal and Khulna clips contain short silences where the
source was cut, the Dhaka clip is quieter with more background energy between
words, and the Mymensingh and Rajshahi clips carry strong low-frequency energy
from close microphones. None of these differences is dialectal, and all of them
are visible to a classifier.

\subsection{Long-term average spectrum}
\label{sec:data-ltas}

A long-term average spectrum (LTAS) averages the power spectrum of speech over
many seconds, removing the phonetic detail of individual sounds and leaving the
gross spectral shape that voice quality, speaking style and the recording
channel impose. \Cref{fig:ltas} shows the LTAS of each class against the corpus
mean. Each curve is the Welch power spectral density of every clip in the class
(1,024-point Hann windows, 50\,\% overlap), normalised to unit total power so
that recording level does not dominate, averaged within the class and plotted
in decibels; the corpus-wide average is drawn in grey behind each class for
comparison. \Cref{fig:ltas-diff} shows the same curves as differences from the
corpus mean, with the Standard class highlighted and the eight regional classes
drawn in one colour, because the point of that figure is their spread rather
than their identity.

\begin{figure}[p]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{dataset_ltas.pdf}
  \caption{Long-term average spectrum per class.}
  \label{fig:ltas}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{dataset_ltas_diff.pdf}
  \caption{LTAS of each class relative to the corpus mean.}
  \label{fig:ltas-diff}
\end{figure}

Three observations follow. First, all nine classes share the expected shape of
speech: a peak below 500\,Hz and a roll-off of about 6\,dB per octave above
it, with the sharp drop above 7.6\,kHz that comes from the original
compression of the media. Second, the classes nevertheless differ by up to
$\pm 5$\,dB over broad bands. Standard sits 2-3\,dB below the mean between
1 and 3\,kHz and above it from 4.5\,kHz upward, which is consistent with
studio speech recorded with less low-frequency proximity effect and more
high-frequency clarity than field or drama audio. Third, two regional classes,
Barishal and Noakhali, show a fine periodic ripple between 4 and 7\,kHz that
no speech process produces; it is the signature of a codec or a particular
recording chain shared by many clips of those classes. The LTAS therefore
records something the thesis has to take seriously: part of what separates the
classes acoustically is the channel they were recorded through, not the
dialect spoken. A classifier can learn such channel signatures, and a clip-level
split rewards it for doing so. This is the empirical motivation for the strict
split of \cref{sec:method-splits} and for the leakage measurement of
\cref{sec:res-strict}.

\section{The Standard class}

Standard is a speech register, the standard, prescribed Bangla of news reading
and formal speech, rather than a region.\footnote{In the released split files,
prediction tables and audio folders this class carries its original label,
\emph{Formal}; the thesis calls it Standard throughout.} It is kept as a ninth
class deliberately. Distinguishing regionally marked speech from the standard
register is of practical interest in its own right, and Standard is the only
class with recoverable provenance, which makes it the calibration anchor for the
strict split (\cref{sec:method-splits}). A consequence is that the class
boundary is partly one of register: Standard speakers may come from any region,
and some regional clips contain stretches of near-standard speech.

\section{Metadata and the leakage problem}
\label{sec:data-metadata}

The corpus ships no speaker labels and almost no provenance. Regional clips have
sequential file names, a district code followed by a running number, that say
nothing about speaker or source. Only the Standard class carries recoverable
source identifiers: 557 of its 1,652 clips embed a YouTube video identifier in
the file name, from 35 distinct videos. For the remaining 8,746 clips, 94\,\% of
the corpus, nothing prevents clips of the same speaker, or from the same
programme and recording channel, from appearing on both sides of a random
train/test split. \Cref{sec:method-splits} describes how the thesis estimates
pseudo-speaker groups to control this, and \cref{sec:res-strict} measures its
effect.

\Cref{fig:speaker-groups} previews the result of that estimation: panel (a)
shows the distribution of pseudo-speaker group sizes and panel (b) the number of
groups per class. The 1,683 groups are very unequal in size: 760 are single
clips, the median group has two, and the largest has 251. The number of groups
per class ranges from 82 (Khulna) to 344 (Noakhali), which is why some strict
test cells are dominated by one cluster.

  \centering

The clips were collected from films and dramas as well as from news and
interviews, so some speakers may be actors performing a dialect rather than
native speakers of it. This cannot be checked from the metadata and is listed
among the limitations (\cref{sec:disc-limits}).

\section{External resources for the lexical route}
\label{sec:data-external}

The lexical route (\cref{sec:method-lexical}) uses three public text and speech
resources in addition to the corpus. None contributes training audio to any
dialect classifier.

\begin{description}
  \item[OOD-Speech] \cite{Rakib2023OODSpeech}: the 963,636 training sentences
    of standard Bangla, distributed with the 2023 Bengali.AI competition
    \cite{BengaliAI2023Speech}, define the standard-Bangla vocabulary.
  \item[Ben-10] \cite{Dipto2025ASR}: the district-labelled dialect
    transcripts of its training set (13,342 clips) contribute regional words for
    the three districts it shares with this corpus (Barishal, Chattogram,
    Sylhet); its public validation set (1,666 clips with audio and human
    transcripts) is the reference for measuring speech-recognition quality.
    Released under CC0.
  \item[Vashantor] \cite{Faria2023Vashantor}: regional-to-standard sentence
    pairs for Chittagong, Noakhali, Sylhet, Barishal and Mymensingh contribute
    regional words for those five classes. Released under CC BY 4.0.
\end{description}
