\chapter{DATASET}
\label{ch:dataset}

\section{Source and composition}

The corpus was gathered for two earlier studies of Bangla accent
classification, Badhon \etal~\cite{Badhon2020Bengali} and Alam \etal~\cite{Alam2023Bangla}, and is
used here with the same class labels. It consists of short clips of Bangla
speech cut from television shows, films and online video, originally
distributed as MP4 and grouped into one folder per class. It contains nine classes: eight regional dialects named after
the district or division they are associated with, and the standard register,
labelled Formal (\cref{tab:classes}). After validation, 9,303 clips totalling
about 13.1 hours are usable; two duplicate \path{.ogg} files in the Formal
folder are excluded. Class sizes range from 750 (Khulna) to 1,652 (Formal).
\Cref{fig:data-pipeline} summarises how the raw clips become the committed
splits used by every experiment, and \cref{fig:class-sizes} shows the class
balance in clips and in hours: Formal is the largest class in both, and the
eight regional classes range from about one hour (Khulna, Dhaka) to 1.75 hours
(Barishal).

\section{Audio properties}

Every clip is standardised to 16\,kHz, mono, 16-bit PCM WAV
(\path{build_manifest.py}). Clips are nominally five seconds long: the median
duration is 5.0\,s and the mean 5.08\,s, but 1,067 clips fall outside
4.5--5.5\,s, with a range of 1.1--8.0\,s; Formal clips are slightly longer
(median 5.6\,s). Five seconds is enough for a listener to recognise many
dialects but excludes longer-range cues such as discourse markers and phrase-level
intonation. Because the material is broadcast and online media, recording
conditions vary widely within and across classes, from studio news to
outdoor drama scenes. \Cref{fig:durations} shows the duration distribution:
every regional clip is exactly 5.0\,s, so the spread in panel (a) and the only
visible box in panel (b) come from the Formal class, whose clips were cut to
sentence boundaries rather than to a fixed length. \Cref{fig:spectrograms}
shows one held-out clip per class as a waveform and an 80-band log-mel
spectrogram, the representation Whisper consumes. The examples already hint at
the variation the models face: the Barishal and Khulna clips contain short
silences where the source was cut, the Dhaka clip is quieter with more
background energy between words, and the Mymensingh and Rajshahi clips carry
strong low-frequency energy from close microphones.

\subsection{Long-term average spectrum}
\label{sec:data-ltas}

A long-term average spectrum (LTAS) averages the power spectrum of speech over
many seconds, removing the phonetic detail of individual sounds and leaving the
gross spectral shape that voice quality, speaking style and the recording
channel impose. \Cref{fig:ltas} shows the LTAS of each class, computed as the
Welch power spectral density of every clip (1,024-point Hann windows, 50\,\%
overlap) normalised to unit total power so that recording level does not
dominate, averaged within the class, and plotted in decibels against the
corpus-wide average. \Cref{fig:ltas-diff} shows the same curves as differences
from the corpus mean.

\begin{figure}[p]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{dataset_ltas.pdf}
  \caption{Long-term average spectrum per class (blue) against the corpus mean
  (grey). Each clip's Welch PSD is normalised to unit power before averaging.}
  \label{fig:ltas}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{dataset_ltas_diff.pdf}
  \caption{Each class's LTAS minus the corpus mean. Formal is highlighted;
  the eight regional classes are drawn in the same colour because the point is
  their spread, not their identity.}
  \label{fig:ltas-diff}
\end{figure}

Three observations follow. First, all nine classes share the expected shape of
speech: a peak below 500\,Hz and a roll-off of about 6\,dB per octave above
it, with the sharp drop above 7.6\,kHz that comes from the original
compression of the media. Second, the classes nevertheless differ by up to
$\pm 5$\,dB over broad bands. Formal sits 2--3\,dB below the mean between
1 and 3\,kHz and above it from 4.5\,kHz upward, which is consistent with
studio speech recorded with less low-frequency proximity effect and more
high-frequency clarity than field or drama audio. Third, two regional classes,
Barishal and Noakhali, show a fine periodic ripple between 4 and 7\,kHz that
no speech process produces; it is the signature of a codec or a particular
recording chain shared by many clips of those classes. The LTAS therefore
records something the thesis has to take seriously: part of what separates the
classes acoustically is the channel they were recorded through, not the
dialect spoken. A classifier can learn such channel signatures, and a clip-level
split rewards it for doing so. This is the empirical motivation for the strict
split of \cref{sec:method-splits} and for the leakage measurement of
\cref{sec:res-strict}.

\section{The Formal class}

Formal is a speech register---the standard, prescribed Bangla of news reading
and formal speech---rather than a region. It is kept as a ninth class
deliberately. Distinguishing regionally marked speech from the standard register
is of practical interest in its own right, and Formal is the only class with
recoverable provenance, which makes it the calibration anchor for the strict
split (\cref{sec:method-splits}). A consequence is that the class boundary is
partly one of register: Formal speakers may come from any region, and some
regional clips contain stretches of near-standard speech.

\section{Metadata and the leakage problem}
\label{sec:data-metadata}

The corpus ships no speaker labels and almost no provenance. Regional clips have
sequential filenames (\path{khl271.wav}, \path{shlt479.wav}) that say
nothing about speaker or source. Only the Formal class carries recoverable
source identifiers: 557 of its 1,652 clips embed a YouTube video identifier in
the filename, from 35 distinct videos. For the remaining 8,746 clips---94\,\% of
the corpus---nothing prevents clips of the same speaker, or from the same
programme and recording channel, from appearing on both sides of a random
train/test split. \Cref{sec:method-splits} describes how the thesis estimates
pseudo-speaker groups to control this, and \cref{sec:res-strict} measures its
effect. \Cref{fig:speaker-groups} previews the result of that estimation: the
1,683 pseudo-speaker groups are very unequal in size---760 are single clips,
the median group has two, and the largest has 251---and the number of groups
per class ranges from 82 (Khulna) to 344 (Noakhali), which is why some strict
test cells are dominated by one cluster.

The clips were collected from films and dramas as well as from news and
interviews, so some speakers may be actors performing a dialect rather than
native speakers of it. This cannot be checked from the metadata and is listed
among the limitations (\cref{sec:disc-limits}).

\section{External resources for the lexical route}
\label{sec:data-external}

The lexical route (\cref{sec:method-lexical}) uses three public text and speech
resources in addition to the corpus. None contributes training audio to any
dialect classifier.

\begin{description}
  \item[OOD-Speech] \cite{Rakib2023OODSpeech}: the 963,636 training sentences
    of standard Bangla, distributed with the 2023 Bengali.AI competition
    \cite{BengaliAI2023Speech}, define the standard-Bangla vocabulary.
  \item[Ben-10] \cite{Dipto2025ASR}: the district-labelled dialect
    transcripts of its training set (13,342 clips) contribute regional words for
    the three districts it shares with this corpus (Barishal, Chattogram,
    Sylhet); its public validation set (1,666 clips with audio and human
    transcripts) is the reference for measuring speech-recognition quality.
    Released under CC0.
  \item[Vashantor] \cite{Faria2023Vashantor}: regional--standard sentence
    pairs for Chittagong, Noakhali, Sylhet, Barishal and Mymensingh contribute
    regional words for those five classes. Released under CC BY 4.0.
\end{description}
