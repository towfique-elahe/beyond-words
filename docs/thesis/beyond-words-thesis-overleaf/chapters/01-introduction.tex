\chapter{INTRODUCTION}
\label{ch:intro}

Bangla is one language with many voices. A speaker from Sylhet, one from
Chattogram and one from Barishal each speak recognisably differently from one
another and from the standard register heard on national news, in pronunciation,
in rhythm and intonation, and in vocabulary. To a listener from Bangladesh these
differences are often audible within a few seconds. For speech technology they
matter twice: dialect is a source of error for speech recognisers trained on
standard speech, and it is information in its own right, useful for routing
calls, adapting recognisers, or studying language variation at scale. Automatic
\emph{dialect identification} (DID) is the task of predicting a speaker's
dialect from their speech. It sits between language identification, where the
classes are different languages, and speaker identification, where the classes
are individuals~\cite{Snyder2018Xvectors}. For widely studied languages such as
Arabic it has dedicated benchmarks~\cite{Ali2019MGB5}; for Bangla it does not.
Bangla speech processing is low-resource in general~\cite{Mridha2021Challenges},
and previous Bangla dialect work has relied on hand-crafted acoustic features or
on written dialect text~\cite{Badhon2020Bengali,Alam2023Bangla,Sadiq2026Bangla}.
Two developments make the task newly tractable. Self-supervised and weakly
supervised speech models---wav2vec~2.0, XLS-R and
Whisper~\cite{Baevski2020wav2vec,Babu2022XLSR,Radford2023Robust}---learn general
representations from very large amounts of multilingual audio, including
Bangla, and transfer to new tasks with little labelled
data~\cite{Yang2021SUPERB}. And public Bangla speech resources have grown: a
1,178-hour corpus of standard Bangla~\cite{Rakib2023OODSpeech} and, recently,
corpora of regional speech with transcripts~\cite{Dipto2025ASR,Hassan2025RegSpeech12}.

This thesis classifies 5-second audio clips into nine classes: eight regional
dialects (Barishal, Chattogram, Dhaka, Khulna, Mymensingh, Noakhali, Rajshahi,
Sylhet) and the standard register, labelled \emph{Formal}. The corpus contains
9,303 clips, about 13.1 hours, collected from television, film and online media
for two earlier studies of Bangla accent
classification~\cite{Badhon2020Bengali,Alam2023Bangla} (\cref{ch:dataset}).
Found data of this kind brings a specific risk. The corpus has no speaker
labels, and for most clips no record of which programme or recording they came
from. A random split can therefore place clips of the same speaker, recorded
with the same microphone in the same room, in both the training and the test
set. A model can then score well by recognising the speaker or the recording
channel rather than the dialect. This is a known failure mode of audio
classification on found data~\cite{Geirhos2020Shortcut,Shulman2026EchoHawk},
but it is usually disclosed as a caveat rather than measured. Measuring it is a
central aim of this thesis.

Dialect is carried by both sound and words. Some cues are acoustic: vowel
quality, consonant realisation, pitch contour and tempo. Others are lexical: a
Barishal speaker may say \bn{mui}{মুই} where standard Bangla has \bn{ami}{আমি}
(``I''); a Sylhet speaker asks \bn{kita}{কিতা} where the standard form is
\bn{ki}{কী} (``what''). The acoustic models used in most of this thesis see only
the sound: they consume log-mel spectrogram frames (Whisper) or the raw waveform
(XLS-R) and have no access to what was said. The thesis therefore compares two
routes. The \textbf{acoustic route} learns dialect from the audio signal with
hand-crafted features, frozen pretrained embeddings and end-to-end fine-tuned
encoders. The \textbf{lexical route} transcribes each clip with off-the-shelf
Bangla speech recognition, separates regional words from standard ones, and
identifies the dialect from the words. Combining the two answers the question
that gives the thesis its title: how much of Bangla dialect identity is carried
by the words, and how much lies beyond them?

The work is organised around four research questions. \textbf{RQ1:} how
accurately can pretrained speech models identify nine Bangla dialect classes
from 5-second clips, compared with hand-crafted acoustic features, and which
encoder is best suited to the task? \textbf{RQ2:} how much of that accuracy
comes from speaker and recording-channel overlap between training and test
data, and do the conclusions survive a speaker-disjoint evaluation?
\textbf{RQ3:} how much dialect information survives off-the-shelf Bangla speech
recognition, and how well can the dialect be identified from the resulting words
alone? \textbf{RQ4:} are the acoustic and lexical routes complementary?

The main contributions of this thesis are the following. First, a 9-class,
13.1-hour Bangla dialect corpus pipeline with validation, standardisation and
leakage-aware splitting, released with its exact train/validation/test splits.
Second, an application of self-supervised and weakly supervised multilingual
speech models (Whisper, XLS-R) to Bangla regional dialect identification,
compared with a hand-crafted baseline under one evaluation protocol. Third, a
measurement of speaker and channel leakage: a strict split built from
ECAPA-TDNN pseudo-speaker clusters, calibrated against the known sources of the
Formal clips, turns the usual ``upper bound'' disclaimer into a measured effect
of 12--14 macro-F1 points for the first pretrained models. Fourth, a comparison
of eight acoustic models under both protocols, with speaker-group bootstrap
confidence intervals and a data-efficiency analysis; fine-tuned Whisper-medium
raises the honest headline to 0.92 with half the leakage drop of XLS-R, and
Bangla-specific pretraining helps a frozen encoder but adds nothing once it is
fine-tuned. Fifth, a negative result: channel-suppression augmentation lowers
strict-split accuracy, which the thesis attributes to prosody being dialect
signal rather than nuisance. Sixth, the lexical route: five off-the-shelf
Bangla speech recognisers compared by how much dialect they preserve, using an
automated dialect-word recall over 1,666 human-transcribed clips;
regional-word matching with an interpretable per-region match percentage; a
text classifier; and acoustic--lexical fusion, which helps a weak acoustic model
(0.808 to 0.856) but not a strong one. Finally, the best model is released as a
public demonstration in which a user records or uploads speech and receives the
predicted dialect.

The remainder of the thesis is organised as follows. \Cref{ch:background}
